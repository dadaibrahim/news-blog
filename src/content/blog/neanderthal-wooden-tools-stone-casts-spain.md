Archaeology has a material bias, and it is not subtle. Stone survives; wood does not. That means our entire picture of Paleolithic life is built from the fraction of material culture that refused to rot, which is a bit like reconstructing modern computing from keyboard casings alone. A newly described discovery from a Spanish cave scrambles that bias in a satisfying way: wooden tools made by Neanderthals that no longer exist as wood at all. Mineral precipitated from groundwater encased the objects while they were buried, the wood itself decayed away, and the shapes remained — tool designs preserved as stone.

## The dark matter of the Stone Age

Wood almost certainly dominated Paleolithic technology the way plastic dominates ours. Spears, digging sticks, hafts, handles, traps, containers: an entire infrastructure of organic material that we mostly cannot see. The handful of wooden artifacts that have survived — the 400,000-year-old point from Clacton in England, the famous Schöningen spears from Germany — come from waterlogged, oxygen-poor deposits that halted decay. Those finds are the exceptions that prove the rule. Dry caves, which preserve bone and charcoal beautifully, are normally hostile to wood. So most of Paleolithic material culture is effectively dark matter: inferred from fracture patterns on stone tools, from wear traces, from residues, but rarely seen directly.

## Nature's lost-wax foundry

The preservation pathway here is elegantly weird. Groundwater percolating through carbonate bedrock dissolves small amounts of it. When that water reaches a new chemical environment — losing carbon dioxide, evaporating, mixing with other waters — the dissolved minerals precipitate back out. In cave sediments, that precipitation can slowly build a hard mineral shell around buried wood. Sealed inside its stone cocoon, the wood eventually decomposed anyway, consumed by microbes over millennia. What is left is a cast or mold: the negative space of a tool, rendered in rock.

Anyone who has read about metal casting will recognize the logic. This is nature running a lost-wax process on geological timescales, except the pattern was a spear shaft and the foundry was a cave floor. For a software audience: think of it as a backup format with extreme lossy compression. The material is gone; the geometry survives.

## Dating the wrapper, not the contents

There is a genuinely clever analytical payoff. The mineral shell itself can be dated. Uranium-series dating measures the decay of uranium isotopes trapped in the precipitate as it formed, bracketing when the encrustation happened. Combine that with the surrounding sediment layers and any stone artifacts in the same deposits, and you get a defensible age window — one that lands squarely in the Neanderthal occupation of Iberia. Dating the wrapper rather than the contents sounds backwards, but it works: the stone shell formed after the wood was buried and before anything disturbed the sequence.

## Reading shapes with modern instruments

This is where the story gets interesting for a technical readership, because the casts are essentially datasets waiting to be interrogated. Micro-CT and structured-light scanning convert them into high-resolution meshes. Geometric morphometrics can then quantify taper, curvature, and symmetry, and compare those signatures against wooden tools recovered from waterlogged European sites. A deliberately shaped point — thinned, symmetrical, tapered — is hard to explain by accident, and harder still to attribute to beavers or roots, which produce convincing-looking pseudo-artifacts that have fooled researchers before. Shape analysis is exactly the kind of tool that separates design from noise.

The limits are real, though. A cast preserves external form, not internal anatomy: no growth rings, no species identification, no surface microwear, no residues. Function remains an inference, not a fact. The honest framing is that these objects constrain the space of possibilities rather than settling it.

## Why one cave changes the census

Every new preservation pathway changes the sample. Hafted stone tools and adhesive residues already told us Neanderthals assembled multi-part implements — wood, stone, hide, pitch — which is systems engineering with flint. Wooden casts add direct evidence for the organic components themselves, the parts of the assembly that were probably the most numerous and the least documented.

It also points at museum drawers. Concretions and encrusted lumps sit in collections across Europe, catalogued decades ago as geological curiosities. Scanning them with modern CT and running shape-classification models over the results is cheap relative to excavation, and there is no telling how many stone-wrapped ghosts are already sitting on shelves.

## The takeaway

Preservation is sampling, and every dataset has bias. The deep past is incomplete in ways we are only now learning to model, and each newly recognized preservation route — bogs, permafrost, and now mineral casting — adds rows to a table we assumed was fixed. Somewhere in Spain, groundwater did what no archivist could: it backed up an organic technology in stone. The wood is gone. The design survived.
