## A New Player in the Retro Handheld Arena

The retro handheld market has long been a tale of two extremes: you have cheap, emulation-based devices that play everything under the sun with varying degrees of accuracy, and you have premium FPGA-based hardware like the Analogue Pocket that nails authenticity but costs a small fortune. FunnyPlaying, a company best known for its aftermarket Game Boy and Game Boy Advance upgrade kits, has just thrown a new option into the ring. The FPGB Mini is a compact handheld that plays original Game Boy and Game Boy Color cartridges—and it's doing so with a price and size that directly challenge the status quo.

## Smaller, Cheaper, and Clearly Focused

Make no mistake: the FPGB Mini is not trying to be an Analogue Pocket killer. It's a purpose-built machine for a specific slice of retro history. While the Pocket is a veritable Swiss Army knife of handheld gaming—supporting Game Boy, Game Boy Color, Game Boy Advance, and, with adapters, a swath of other systems—the FPGB Mini is laser-focused on the original monochrome and color eras. That means you won't be slotting in a GBA cartridge here. What you lose in system compatibility, you gain in simplicity and, presumably, affordability.

FunnyPlaying hasn't formally announced the price yet, but the company's reputation suggests it will be a fraction of the Pocket's $219 asking price. And "mini" isn't just a marketing flourish—the device is noticeably smaller than Analogue's offering, making it far easier to slip into a pocket (pun fully intended) or stash in a bag. For gamers who want a dedicated Game Boy experience without the bulk, that's a significant selling point.

## The FPGA Factor

One of the most intriguing aspects of the FPGB Mini is that it appears to be based on FPGA technology, much like the Pocket. For the uninitiated, an FPGA (field-programmable gate array) works by physically recreating the original hardware's logic circuits, rather than emulating them in software. This results in cycle-perfect accuracy, eliminating many of the timing, audio, and visual glitches that can plague software emulators. For purists who have been spoiled by the Pocket's flawless rendering of titles like *Pokémon Yellow* or *The Legend of Zelda: Link's Awakening*, this is a crucial detail.

FunnyPlaying isn't a newcomer to this kind of engineering. The company has spent years producing replacement motherboards, IPS display kits, and fully custom shells that push original Game Boys to their limits. That expertise shows in the FPGB Mini's design philosophy: it's not about flashy extras, but about getting the core experience right. The LCD is likely to be a high-quality IPS panel with adjustable brightness and sharp pixel response, similar to what the company offers in its mod kits. In other words, you're getting a modernized viewing experience without sacrificing the authentic feel of the original hardware.

## Who Should Buy This?

Let's be honest: the Analogue Pocket has a lot of appeal, but it's also overkill for many players. If you grew up with a Game Boy Color and your beloved cartridge collection is limited to that era, paying $219 to play them—especially when you don't care about GBA or other systems—feels excessive. The FPGB Mini seems designed precisely for that audience. It's also a wonderful entry point for newcomers who want to explore classic Game Boy games without investing heavily in brittle original hardware or navigating the murky waters of cheap emulation handhelds.

Of course, there are trade-offs. The lack of GBA support is the most obvious one, but there's also the question of build quality and software polish. Analogue has spent years refining the Pocket's firmware and UI, and its device has a luxurious aluminum shell. The FPGB Mini, being a budget product, will likely use plastic and offer a more utilitarian interface. Then there's the second-hand market: aftermarket parts and custom shells from FunnyPlaying are often excellent, but they're not always as refined as a premium product built from the ground up.

## The Bigger Picture

FunnyPlaying's entry into the handheld market is significant because it signals a shift in the retro gaming landscape. FPGA technology was once the exclusive province of boutique manufacturers like Analogue and MiSTer enthusiasts who were willing to tinker. By bringing it to a smaller, more affordable form factor, FunnyPlaying is democratizing FPGA accuracy. This could pressure other companies to lower their prices or, alternatively, inspire a wave of budget-friendly FPGA devices that prioritize core compatibility over broad system support.

For now, the FPGB Mini is shaping up to be a niche product for a nostalgic audience. But it's a niche that has proven time and again that it will spend money on well-made hardware. If FunnyPlaying can deliver on the promise of accurate gameplay, a bright screen, and cartridge compatibility for under $100, the device could easily become the go-to recommendation for Game Boy fans on a budget. It's not trying to be everything to everyone, and that's exactly what makes it so promising.

## Final Thoughts

The FPGB Mini isn't a revolutionary concept—it's a carefully focused execution of an idea that retro gamers have been asking for: a cheap, accurate, and compact way to play their classic cartridges. While it won't replace an Analogue Pocket for those who want a multi-system workhorse, it could very well become the everyday carry for purists who care more about the original Game Boy experience than about bragging rights. Keep an eye on this one; it might just be the handheld that finally makes FPGA gaming mainstream.
